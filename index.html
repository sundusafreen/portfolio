I didn't start in a data lab. I started in the field, managing government and enterprise accounts, sitting across the table from engineers and executives at BHEL and NTPC who didn't want a pitch. They wanted evidence.

That job taught me something no classroom really could: business decisions almost never happen inside a clean spreadsheet. There are people involved, politics, deadlines, half-finished information, and someone who has to decide anyway.

"I didn't choose analytics to escape business. I chose it to understand it more precisely."

Going back for an MSc in Business Analytics at Trinity College Dublin wasn't an exit from business. It was me trying to formalise what I'd already learned the hard way: the gap between a good strategy and a great one is almost always a data problem.

My edge, if I have one, is that I've sat on both sides of this. I know what a stakeholder needs from an answer because I used to be the one waiting on it, and now I'm learning to build that answer myself.

03 How I think

The mental model I run
every time.

Click a step. okay, but WHY?

There's usually a better question hiding under the one you were first asked. Before I open a dataset, I try to separate the symptom someone noticed from the root cause underneath it.

Numbers need context. The same metric means something different depending on who's making the decision and what they're constrained by, so I spend time with stakeholders before I spend time with data.

What data exists, what's missing, and what's just noisy. I write down assumptions and quality risks upfront, because that's cheaper than finding them halfway through an analysis.

This is where it gets interesting: patterns are easy to find, explanations are hard to earn. I try to rule out the boring reason before I let myself get excited about an interesting one.

Insight ≠ action. I build every analysis backwards from the decision it's supposed to support. If I can't say what someone should do differently, I'm not done yet.

The loop doesn't close at the recommendation. Where I've been able to, I've gone back and checked whether the decision actually moved the number it was meant to.
